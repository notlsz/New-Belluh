# Theory and Evidence: Why Local Firms Beat Global Entrants, and Single vs Simultaneous Multi-Market Expansion

Source-quality legend used below:
- [V] = verified this session from the linked page (abstract, summary, or full text).
- [R] = classic finding recalled from the literature. The link is the canonical paper page, but the specific number or detail was NOT re-verified this session. The report writer should treat [R] items as needing a check before quoting numbers.

## 1. Liability of foreignness (Zaheer 1995) and liability of outsidership (Johanson & Vahlne 2009)

### Takeaway
Foreign entrants face real, measurable extra costs (unfamiliarity, discrimination, missing networks) that show up as lower profitability and higher exit rates. The cost falls with time in market. The revised Uppsala model moves the problem from "being foreign" to "being outside the relevant network": you win by getting inside local business networks, not just by being in the country.

### Cited Findings
- Zaheer (1995, Academy of Management Journal 38(2)) coined "liability of foreignness" using matched currency trading rooms of US and Japanese banks in New York and Tokyo; foreign rooms were less profitable than local ones, and importing home-country organizational capabilities helped more than copying local practices. [R] — [Zaheer 1995 record](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/363808)
- Zaheer & Mosakowski (1997, SMJ 18(6)) studied survival of currency trading rooms worldwide (roughly 2,600 rooms, 47 countries, 1974 to 1993); foreign rooms had higher exit rates, and the liability declined over time, largely disappearing after about 16 years in market. [R: numbers from memory] — [Zaheer & Mosakowski 1997, IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v18y1997i6p439-463.html); [UMN record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/the-dynamics-of-the-liability-of-foreignness-a-global-study-of-su/)
- Later work deconstructs the liability: e.g. regulators take more enforcement actions against foreign banks than comparable domestic ones (discrimination component), and some studies find an "advantage of foreignness" in certain categories. [V titles only] — [Deconstructing LOF: enforcement actions against foreign banks](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/370364); [The advantage of foreignness](https://theses.ubn.ru.nl/items/cfceb8f1-2d97-4e6f-b1c6-23f7c11043a3); [Foreignness research review](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/369526)
- Miller & Eden (2006, AMJ) found the liability of foreignness for foreign banks in the US varied with the cultural distance of the home country (local isomorphism reduces it). [R] — [Miller & Eden 2006 PDF](https://www.voxprof.com/eden/Publications/miller-eden-amj-2006.pdf)
- Johanson & Vahlne (2009, JIBS 40) revised the Uppsala model: markets are networks of relationships; the core handicap is "liability of outsidership" (not being an insider in the relevant network), not foreignness per se; internationalization proceeds by building trust, commitment and knowledge inside networks, and firms follow partners into markets. Psychic distance still matters but mainly through its effect on network building. [R] — [Johanson & Vahlne 2009, JIBS (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1057/jibs.2009.24)

### Inferences
- For a startup, "local" is a network position you can buy or build: local co-founders, local design partners, local channel partners and local reference customers reduce outsidership faster than translation alone.
- The liability decays with time, so early market presence is an asset; but the decay in the classic data took years, not quarters.

- Could not fetch full texts of Zaheer 1995 or Zaheer & Mosakowski 1997 (captcha/paywall), so effect sizes and exact sample counts are unverified.
- Found no meta-analysis specifically quantifying liability of foreignness for software or AI firms.

## 2. Ghemawat: CAGE distance, Semiglobalization and AAA

### Takeaway
Cross-border activity is far less than full globalization ("semiglobalization"), and Cultural, Administrative, Geographic and Economic distance each sharply reduce trade and investment. For software/AI, geographic distance shrinks but cultural (language, trust norms) and administrative (data residency, AI regulation, procurement rules) distance grow in importance.

### Cited Findings
- "Distance Still Matters" (HBR 2001) introduced CAGE; citing gravity-model research, it reports trade between countries that share a language is about 42% higher, a colonial tie about 188% higher, a common regional trading bloc about 47% higher, and a common currency about 340% higher than otherwise. [R: numbers from memory of the article] — [Ghemawat 2001, HBR](https://hbr.org/2001/09/distance-still-matters-the-hard-reality-of-global-expansion)
- "Managing Differences: The Central Challenge of Global Strategy" (HBR 2007) defines AAA: Adaptation (adjust to local differences), Aggregation (group regions/segments to get scale), Arbitrage (exploit differences, e.g. labor cost). Ghemawat argues firms rarely do all three well and should pick one or two. [R] — [Ghemawat 2007, HBR](https://hbr.org/2007/03/managing-differences-the-central-challenge-of-global-strategy)
- Independent gravity-model work continues to find distance, common language and colonial links significant for trade flows. [V titles] — [PIIE gravity chapter](https://piie.com/publications/chapters_preview/72/6iie2024.pdf); [CORE gravity paper](https://core.ac.uk/download/80912579.pdf)

### Inferences
- AI/software application: Aggregation = build one core model/platform and group countries into clusters by language and regulation (e.g. EU AI Act bloc, Spanish-speaking LatAm, GCC); Adaptation = locale-specific UX, payment rails, compliance packs; Arbitrage = cheap engineering/labelling hubs. A startup should usually lead with Aggregation plus targeted Adaptation.
- Administrative distance (data localization, AI rules) is the CAGE dimension most likely to grow for AI companies.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found applying CAGE quantitatively to SaaS or AI product adoption.

## 3. Khanna & Palepu: institutional voids

### Takeaway
Emerging markets lack intermediaries (credit bureaus, payment rails, logistics, certification, legal enforcement). Locals who fill or work around these voids build moats that foreign entrants, who assume the voids are filled, ignore.

### Cited Findings
- Khanna & Palepu (HBR 1997 "Why Focused Strategies May Be Wrong for Emerging Markets"; HBR 2005 "Strategies That Fit Emerging Markets"; book "Winning in Emerging Markets" 2010) argue firms must map voids in product, capital and labor markets plus political and social systems, and either adapt, work around, or fill the void as a business. [R] — [Khanna, Palepu & Sinha 2005, HBR](https://hbr.org/2005/06/strategies-that-fit-emerging-markets)
- Bhattacharya & Michael (HBR 2008) examples show voids being filled: Focus Media (China) staff cycled between buildings swapping ad DVDs on screens at 130,000 locations in 90 cities rather than networking them; Shanda built online games as live services, sidestepping piracy (an institutional void in IP enforcement). [V] — [CBS/HBR excerpt](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/how-local-companies-keep-multinationals-at-bay)

### Inferences
- Alipay (escrow to solve trust between strangers), cash-on-delivery in India/SE Asia and M-Pesa are canonical void-filling plays (no source fetched this session; widely documented). For an AI startup, voids include: absent compliance intermediaries, low trust in AI outputs, lack of local-language training data, and missing integration with local apps (WeChat, LINE, Kakao, WhatsApp-first commerce).

### Gaps
- Did not fetch the original HBR texts; no quantitative test of "void-filling predicts local win" was located.

## 4. Dawar & Frost (1999) and Bhattacharya & Michael (2008): tactics of local firms

### Takeaway
Local firms win when they exploit assets multinationals cannot easily copy (local customer knowledge, distribution, cost structure, speed) and when industry pressure to globalize is weak. Specific, repeatable tactics: customize to local budgets and habits, design business models around local obstacles, scale fast nationally, hire top talent.

### Cited Findings
- Dawar & Frost ("Competing with Giants", HBR 1999) use two axes (strength of globalization pressure in the industry; transferability of the local firm's assets abroad) to define four postures: Defender (low pressure, local assets: defend home turf with local-preference advantages), Extender (low pressure, transferable assets: expand into similar markets, e.g. Jollibee following the Filipino diaspora), Dodger (high pressure, local assets: refocus on a niche, become a supplier/partner or sell to a multinational), Contender (high pressure, transferable assets: compete globally in a niche, e.g. India's Sundaram Fasteners). [R] — [Dawar & Frost 1999, HBR](https://hbr.org/1999/03/competing-with-giants-survival-strategies-for-local-companies-in-emerging-markets)
- Bhattacharya & Michael ("How Local Companies Keep Multinationals at Bay", HBR March 2008, BCG) list six tactics: (1) customized products/services (CavinKare's single-use shampoo sachets; largest local player in India's $500M shampoo market); (2) business models that sidestep local obstacles (Shanda's live online games beat piracy); (3) latest technology to cut costs (Gol's single-model young 737 fleet, e-tickets, kiosks); (4) low-cost labor instead of automation (Focus Media); (5) rapid scale-up, often by acquisition, to national coverage (Focus Media overtook two global leaders in Chinese outdoor ads); (6) invest in management talent (Wimm-Bill-Dann hired an experienced CEO and multinational managers; 34% of Russian packaged dairy). [V] — [CBS/HBR excerpt](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/how-local-companies-keep-multinationals-at-bay); [HBR via O'Reilly](https://oreilly.com/library/view/harvard-business-review/9781422172155/chapter004.html)
- The underlying BCG report (2008) found companies from emerging markets were conquering their domestic markets against multinationals. [V title] — [BCG press release, GlobeNewswire 2008](https://www.globenewswire.com/fr/news-release/2008/03/06/924362/0/en/Companies-From-Emerging-Markets-Are-Redefining-the-Rules-of-Competition-and-Conquering-Their-Domestic-Markets-Warns-New-Report-by-The-Boston-Consulting-Group.html)

### Inferences
- A global entrant beats locals by neutralizing these six tactics: price for local budgets, adopt local channels, move fast to national scale in each chosen country, and hire senior local operators.
- These are case-based frameworks (folklore-grade evidence): illustrative, not statistically tested.

### Gaps
- Sample size of companies behind Bhattacharya & Michael not stated in the excerpt; Dawar & Frost is case-based (no stats).

## 5. Born globals / international new ventures

### Takeaway
Early, rapid internationalization is common and viable, but success is driven by specific capabilities (international entrepreneurial orientation, founder international experience and networks, unique/knowledge-intensive products, marketing competence), not by speed itself. A 2025/2026 meta-analysis (378 studies) now provides the best aggregate evidence.

### Cited Findings
- Oviatt & McDougall (1994, JIBS) defined international new ventures as firms that from inception seek advantage from resources and sales in multiple countries; four elements: internalization of some transactions, alternative governance (alliances/networks), foreign location advantage, unique resources. [R] — [Oviatt & McDougall 1994 (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1057/palgrave.jibs.8490193)
- Knight & Cavusgil (2004, JIBS) found born-global performance driven by an international entrepreneurial orientation and international marketing orientation, which produce capabilities such as global technological competence, unique product development, quality focus and leveraging foreign distributors. [R] — [Knight & Cavusgil 2004 (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1057/palgrave.jibs.8400071)
- Fariborzi et al. (2025, Journal of Management Studies 63(6), Sept 2026 issue): meta-analytic SEM of 378 empirical studies and 426 samples; separates individual-level and firm-level antecedents; tests speed, scope and intensity of early internationalization against post-entry performance. [V abstract; effect sizes not visible] — [MetaTOC abstract](https://metatoc.com/papers/202235-early-internationalization-a-meta-analysis-of-antecedents-dimensions-and-performance); [Full text PDF, Reading repository](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/124619/9/J%20Management%20Studies%20-%202025%20-%20Fariborzi%20-%20Early%20Internationalization%20%20A%20Meta%E2%80%90Analysis%20of%20Antecedents%20%20Dimensions%20%20and.pdf)
- An integrated review of born-global performance drivers (Heriot-Watt/Reading) synthesizes entrepreneurial, network and capability drivers. [V title] — [Understanding the drivers of international performance for born global firms](https://researchportal.hw.ac.uk/en/publications/understanding-the-drivers-of-international-performance-for-born-g/)

### Inferences
- For an AI startup: speed of going multi-country is not itself the predictor; founder international networks, a product with a clear knowledge advantage, and strong international marketing capability are. Plan for these before going wide.

### Gaps
- Could not extract the Fariborzi meta-analysis effect sizes (sign and size of speed/scope/intensity effect on performance). The writer should consult the full PDF linked above before stating direction.

## 6. Sprinkler vs waterfall entry

### Takeaway
Theory and the best empirical simulation say sprinkler (simultaneous) entry maximizes revenue when competitors can copy fast, markets are large/similar and cross-country learning is weak, but it carries more risk; waterfall (sequential) minimizes risk and suits markets that need heavy adaptation. Evidence is thin and mostly from consumer durables and pharma, not apps.

### Cited Findings
- Ayal & Zif (1979, Journal of Marketing 43(2)) contrasted market concentration (few markets, deep) vs market spreading (many markets, fast) and listed conditions favoring spreading: low growth per market, short competitive lead time, high spillover between markets, low need for product/communication adaptation, low scale economies in distribution, low program-control needs. [R] — [Ayal & Zif 1979 (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224297904300209)
- Kalish, Mahajan & Muller (1995, IJRM) modeled waterfall vs sprinkler in competitive global markets and concluded that conditions at the time (short competitor lead times, fast diffusion, large foreign markets) increasingly favored sprinkler; waterfall favored when foreign markets are small, slow-growing, entry fixed costs are high, and competitors are slow. [V via Stremersch & Tellis' literature table; detailed conditions R] — [TAU record](https://cris.tau.ac.il/en/publications/waterfall-and-sprinkler-new-product-strategies-in-competitive-glo/); [Stremersch & Tellis 2004 PDF](https://personal.eur.nl/stremersch/artikels/Understanding%20and%20managing%20international%20growth%20of%20new%20products.pdf)
- Stremersch & Tellis (2004, IJRM): 10 consumer durables, 16 European countries, 114 country-category pairs, 1950 to 2000. Simulation: sprinkler generated more sales because the market-size effect outweighs a small cross-country learning effect, but had the highest risk index; a North-to-South waterfall (Nordic, then mid-Europe, then Mediterranean) had the lowest risk. The introduction lag between countries had a limited, non-robust effect on growth. [V] — [Stremersch & Tellis 2004 PDF](https://personal.eur.nl/stremersch/artikels/Understanding%20and%20managing%20international%20growth%20of%20new%20products.pdf)
- Tellis, Stremersch & Yin (2003, as cited): new products take off on average about 6 years after introduction in European countries; time to takeoff in Scandinavia is roughly half that in Mediterranean countries; culture explains takeoff differences better than economics, while growth after takeoff is driven more by wealth. [V as cited] — [Stremersch & Tellis 2004 PDF](https://personal.eur.nl/stremersch/artikels/Understanding%20and%20managing%20international%20growth%20of%20new%20products.pdf); [INFORMS release 2003](https://www.informs.org/News-Room/INFORMS-Releases/News-Releases/EUROPEAN-ECONOMIES-SHOW-DRAMATIC-DIFFERENCES-IN-INNOVATIVENESS-SAYS-OPERATIONS-RESEARCH-STUDY-August-1-2003)
- Prior work: Kumar & Krishnan (2002) found lead-lag and simultaneous cross-country diffusion effects moderated by cultural/economic similarity; Takada & Jain (1991) found the imitation coefficient rises with the introduction lag (later countries adopt faster). [V as cited] — [Stremersch & Tellis 2004 PDF](https://personal.eur.nl/stremersch/artikels/Understanding%20and%20managing%20international%20growth%20of%20new%20products.pdf)
- Fischer, Shankar & Clement (MSI 2005; pharma data) examined whether international market scope and rollout speed moderate order-of-entry and marketing-spend effects on share, i.e. whether a late mover can use a fast, broad rollout to challenge a pioneer. [V abstract only] — [MSI working paper](https://www.msi.org/working-papers/can-a-late-mover-use-international-market-entry-strategy-to-challenge-the-pioneer/)
- A 2022/2023 International Marketing Review study tests waterfall vs sprinkler among Korean SMEs launching new products abroad. [V title only] — [Emerald IMR-05-2022-0106](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/IMR-05-2022-0106/full/html); [AMiner record](https://www.aminer.org/pub/63a046ef90e50fcafde73127/launching-new-products-in-international-markets-waterfall-versus-sprinkler-strategy-of-korean)

### Inferences
- When simultaneous entry wins (synthesis): low adaptation cost per market (digital, language handled by AI), short competitor lead time, high cross-market spillover (global word of mouth, global network effects), low fixed entry cost. When waterfall wins: high regulatory/compliance adaptation, local network effects requiring density, high fixed go-to-market costs (sales teams), and limited capital.
- A practical hybrid for software: "sprinkler for product availability, waterfall for go-to-market investment" (make the product usable everywhere, then concentrate sales and partnerships on a sequence of clusters). This hybrid is an inference, not a tested finding.

### Gaps
- Found no rigorous empirical study of sprinkler vs waterfall for mobile apps, SaaS or AI products. Full results of the Fischer et al. and Korean SME studies not retrieved.

## 7. Network effects and locality

### Takeaway
Whether a market is "local" depends on the shape of the network: local cross-side networks (ride-hailing, food delivery, local services) fragment into city clusters that a local rival can attack one at a time; global networks (lodging marketplaces, social/messaging where contacts span borders) are harder to attack. Multi-homing and disintermediation erode advantage regardless of scale.

### Cited Findings
- Zhu & Iansiti ("Why Some Platforms Thrive and Others Don't", HBR Jan-Feb 2019) name five network properties: strength of network effects; network clustering; risk of disintermediation; vulnerability to multi-homing; network bridging. Uber's drivers and riders form isolated city clusters, so rivals can attack city by city; Airbnb's hosts and guests form essentially one global cluster, harder to challenge. Alipay bridged with Taobao/Tmall; Amazon counters multi-homing via fulfillment. Conclusion: network properties matter more than scale. [V via summary] — [MAAW summary](https://maaw.info/ArticleSummaries/ArtSumZhuandIansiti2019.htm); [HBR via O'Reilly](https://oreilly.com/library/view/hbrs-10-must/9781633699892/Text/ch08.html)
- Rochet & Tirole (2003, JEEA) formalized two-sided markets: platform pricing structure across sides matters, and multi-homing on one side changes competition and pricing. [R] — [Rochet & Tirole 2003 (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1162/154247603322493212)

### Inferences
- An AI product with mostly direct (data/learning) network effects and few cross-border user-to-user effects behaves "local-ish": each country's data, integrations and compliance are separate clusters, giving locals openings. Building cross-border bridges (shared templates, marketplace of agents, global knowledge graph) makes the network more global.
- Didi forcing Uber out of China (2016) and Grab buying Uber SE Asia (2018) fit the city-cluster prediction (widely reported; not sourced this session).

### Gaps
- Original HBR page was blocked; relied on a secondary summary. No quantitative cross-country platform study retrieved.

## 8. Culture frameworks and whether cultural adaptation measurably causes wins

### Takeaway
Language localization has the strongest, most consistent evidence (large stated-preference effects). Deeper cultural adaptation of sites/UI has small-sample experimental support, with effects concentrated in high-uncertainty-avoidance cultures. Hofstede-type dimensions have modest predictive power (mean individual-level effect about 0.18) and serious critiques; use them as hypotheses to test, not as rules.

### Cited Findings
- CSA Research (2020, "Can't Read, Won't Buy"): 8,709 consumers in 29 countries (vetted from 31,933 by Kantar); 76% prefer buying products with information in their native language; 40% will never buy from websites in other languages; 73% want at least product reviews in their language; 67% tolerate mixed-language sites; 66% use machine translation. Stated preference, not observed behavior; vendor is a localization research firm. [V] — [CSA press release via Newswire](https://www.newswire.com/news/survey-of-8-709-consumers-in-29-countries-finds-that-76-prefer-21174283); [CSA](https://csa-research.com/Blogs-Events/CSA-in-the-Media/Press-Releases/Consumers-Prefer-their-Own-Language)
- Taras, Kirkman & Steel (2010, J. Applied Psychology): meta-analysis of 598 studies, over 200,000 individuals; Hofstede value dimensions relate most strongly to emotions, then attitudes, then behaviors, then job performance; mean absolute individual-level effect ρ = 0.18; effects stronger in culturally "tight" countries; values predict some outcomes worse than personality/demographics. [V] — [U Calgary PRISM abstract](https://prism.ucalgary.ca/items/493650c4-2e30-4994-b2fd-38dc17686197/full)
- McSweeney (2002, Human Relations) critiques Hofstede: single-company (IBM) samples, assumes national culture is uniform and causal, questionable survey-to-nation inference. [R] — [McSweeney 2002 (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018726702551004)
- Reinecke & Bernstein (2013, MIS Quarterly): MOCCA adaptive to-do app; N = 105 (30 multicultural users; 75 students in Thailand 30, Rwanda 21, Switzerland 24). Hofstede-based rules predicted users' preferred UI choices ~61% (chance 33%): Thailand 60.8%, Switzerland 56.8%, Rwanda 24.4% (chance level). Learning from in-country majority choices raised accuracy to ~60% average (Rwanda 54.2%). Paper mockups, preference prediction only; no conversion or task-performance test vs a standard UI. [V] — [Reinecke & Bernstein 2013 PDF](https://wildlab.cs.washington.edu/Publications_files/ReineckeBernstein_MIS-Q_2013.pdf)
- Prior evidence cited by Reinecke & Bernstein: localized interfaces linked to more positive attitudes (Nantel & Glaser 2008), perceived usability (Ford & Gelderblom 2003) and appeal (Corbitt et al. 2002). [V as cited] — [same PDF](https://wildlab.cs.washington.edu/Publications_files/ReineckeBernstein_MIS-Q_2013.pdf)
- Ko, Seo & Jung (2015, Marketing Letters): Korean vs US travel-agency sites; culturally congruent sites raised willingness to pay for Korean (high uncertainty avoidance) consumers via processing fluency; no significant effect for US consumers. [V abstract] — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/mktlet/v26y2015i3p377-390.html)
- Singh, Fassott, Chao & Hoffmann (2006, International Marketing Review) found culturally adapted websites associated with more favorable attitudes and purchase intentions across country samples. [R: detailed numbers not verified] — [Emerald DOI page](https://d2pnhgckm19p0r.cloudfront.net/insight/content/doi/10.1108/02651330610646304/full/html); Singh et al. on localization across Italy, India, Netherlands, Spain, Switzerland — [Emerald](https://emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/1525383X200400004/full/html)
- Cyr (2008, JMIS) found website design elements (information design, navigation, visual design) affect trust, satisfaction and e-loyalty differently across Canada, Germany and China. [R] — [Cyr 2008 (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.2753/MIS0742-1222240402)
- Culture shapes takeoff speed of new products across countries (Tellis et al. 2003, see section 6): an indirect "culture matters for adoption timing" result with large samples. [V as cited] — [Stremersch & Tellis 2004 PDF](https://personal.eur.nl/stremersch/artikels/Understanding%20and%20managing%20international%20growth%20of%20new%20products.pdf)

### Inferences
- Evidence ladder: (1) native language = strong evidence, do it everywhere; (2) local payment, formats, social proof and reviews = strong practitioner/stated evidence; (3) deeper visual/UX cultural adaptation = modest experimental evidence, biggest payoff in high-uncertainty-avoidance markets (Japan, Korea, Germany, France rank high on that dimension per Hofstede); (4) "understanding history/psychology causes wins" = plausible but not causally demonstrated in the literature found. A/B testing per market is the way to turn (3) and (4) into evidence.
- Hall high/low-context and Meyer's Culture Map are practitioner frameworks with little direct outcome evidence (no studies retrieved).

### Gaps
- No field experiment found measuring conversion lift from cultural (beyond language) adaptation at scale. GLOBE, Hall and Meyer evidence not retrieved this session.

## 9. Technology acceptance across cultures and trust in AI by country

### Takeaway
Trust in and use of AI is much higher in emerging economies than in advanced ones, and fell between 2022 and 2024. This inverts the usual assumption that rich markets adopt first: for AI, emerging markets may be more receptive, while advanced markets demand regulation and assurance.

### Cited Findings
- KPMG & University of Melbourne "Trust, attitudes and use of AI: A global study 2025": 48,000 respondents, 47 countries (fieldwork Nov 2024 to Jan 2025). 54% are wary of trusting AI; trust 39% in advanced economies vs 57% in emerging; acceptance 65% vs 84%; perceived trustworthiness of AI fell from 63% (2022) to 56% (2024); 66% use AI regularly (80% emerging vs 58% advanced); 58% of employees use AI at work regularly (31% daily/weekly); 83% of students; fewer than 40% have AI training (50% emerging vs 32% advanced); 70%-plus want regulation (76% international laws, 69% national, 71% co-regulation); only 43% think current regulation adequate; 87% want stronger laws against AI misinformation. [V] — [KPMG executive summary PDF](https://kpmg.com/kpmg-us/content/dam/kpmg/pdf/2025/trust-attitudes-artificial-intelligence-executive-summary.pdf); [Melbourne Business School news](https://mbs.edu/news/Global-study-reveals-trust-of-AI-remains-a-critical-challenge)
- Country-level reports exist (e.g. Belgium, Ireland, Singapore, Thailand) for per-country numbers. [V links] — [Belgium report](https://assets.kpmg.com/content/dam/kpmg/be/pdf/TA-Trust-attitudes-and-use-of-AI_Belgium-full-country-level-report-2025.pdf); [Ireland](https://assets.kpmg.com/content/dam/kpmg/ie/pdf/2025/05/ie-trust-in-ai.pdf); [Singapore](https://kpmg.com/sg/en/home/insights/2025/04/trust-attitudes-and-use-of-ai.html)
- UTAUT (Venkatesh et al. 2003) cross-cultural tests generally find culture moderates the weight of performance expectancy vs social influence (social influence matters more in collectivist cultures). [R; no meta-analysis retrieved] — [Venkatesh et al. 2003 (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.2307/30036540)

### Inferences
- Go-to-market for AI: in emerging markets (India, Nigeria, Brazil, SE Asia) lead with capability and access; in advanced markets (EU, Japan, Australia, US) lead with assurance (certifications, human oversight, data residency, audit trails).

### Gaps
- Did not retrieve country-ranked trust figures (highest/lowest country), Edelman Trust Barometer AI data, or Ipsos AI Monitor 2025/2026 figures. UTAUT cross-cultural meta-analysis not retrieved.

## 10. Glocalization and standardization vs adaptation meta-analyses

### Takeaway
The Levitt (standardize) vs Douglas & Wind (adapt) debate resolves to contingency: in exporter studies adaptation tends to improve performance; in MNC studies whole-program standardization tends to help; product standardization hurts financial performance while promotion standardization helps. Industrial/B2B products tolerate more standardization than consumer products.

### Cited Findings
- Levitt ("The Globalization of Markets", HBR 1983) argued technology homogenizes tastes so firms should sell standardized products globally. [R] — [Levitt 1983, HBR](https://hbr.org/1983/05/the-globalization-of-markets)
- Douglas & Wind (1987, Columbia Journal of World Business, "The Myth of Globalization") argued Levitt's assumptions (homogeneous needs, price over features, scale economies) rarely hold; standardization is one option among several. [R] — [Douglas & Wind 1987 (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-5428(87)90009-7)
- Tan & Sousa (2013, Management International Review): meta-analysis of 110 independent samples from 108 articles; product standardization hurts financial (not strategic) performance; promotion standardization helps both; distribution standardization has no direct effect; standardization more suitable for industrial than consumer products. - Leonidou, Katsikeas & Samiee (2002) meta-analysis (26 studies): adaptation positively related to export performance.
